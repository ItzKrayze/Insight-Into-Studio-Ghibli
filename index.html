QuestionMust a happy ending restore what was lost?

Does a happy ending have to restore what was lost?

press to start the story

Everyone loves a good happy ending. The problem gets sorted, the characters move on, and everything slots back into place. But what if it doesn’t? Can an ending still be happy when something has changed for good?

01Happy endings一

An ending is just where the story stops.

Closure is different. It’s the feeling that the questions the story asked have actually been answered.

Closure, defined
More on Narrative Closure ↗

Funnily enough, the people who study this don’t agree on what closure even is. Some say it’s about the plot landing somewhere clear and settled. Others say the emotions have to settle too. So a film can answer everything and still leave you feeling a bit off.

Closed and open endings aren’t the same as happy and sad ones.

Closed means most things get tied up. Open means some stuff is left for you to figure out. Happy and sad is about how it feels. You can have a happy open ending or a sad closed one, and either can work really well.

Fixed doesn’t mean back to normal.

Winning the big fight doesn’t put everything back how it was. Sometimes a character ends up in the same place they started, but they’re a different person. That’s what I want to dig into here: actually getting something back, versus just going back.

That’s why I picked Kiki’s Delivery Service. Kiki is a young witch who leaves home to live on her own in a new city, and she starts a delivery service using her flying. Then her confidence goes, then her flying, then she can’t understand Jiji anymore. At the end she can fly again, but Jiji’s voice never comes back.

Growing up means leaving some things behind, like the imaginary friend you forget about. It’s sad, but it’s part of life.on Jiji
02How stories work二

Most stories go from one balance to another.

A guy called Tzvetan Todorov said stories start with things being normal. Then something breaks it, people notice, they try to fix it, and things settle again. Scroll through the graph below to watch it happen.

Todorov, 1971
The model explained ↗

Fig. 1 · Todorov’s five stages

close, but never the same
  1. 1 Equilibrium
  2. 2 Disruption
  3. 3 Recognition
  4. 4 Attempt to fix it
  5. 5 New equilibrium

The last stage is the interesting bit. The new balance isn’t the old one. The line gets close to where it started but never lands on it.

Kiki doesn’t really have one clear disruption. Leaving home starts one journey, and losing her flight starts another inside it. Todorov also only explains how a story is built, not why an ending feels good.

Stories keep asking questions.

Noël Carroll looks at it a different way. He says the plot is basically a bunch of questions getting answered. Some are big and run the whole film. Others are small and come from single scenes. Every answer closes off a few possibilities.

  • bigWill she cope on her own?
  • bigWill she find her place in the city?
  • smallWill she get the toy back?
  • smallWill she make it to the party?
  • bigWill she fly again?

Some of Kiki’s questions get answered straight away by what happens. Others are really about who she’s turning into, and those don’t get a neat answer. Carroll’s idea has critics too (Jonathan Frome is one), so I’m treating it as a handy way of looking, not a rule.

So why do we want answers so badly?

03Losing it三

At the start, Kiki is sure of herself.

You can tell from what she does, not what she says. She makes decisions, takes jobs and fixes problems as they pop up. It looks less like being skilled and more like being up for trying anything.

Four small moments

Keep scrolling. This is where her confidence starts to slip.

The cat toy

A delivery goes wrong and she goes back to fix it. She only manages with other people’s help, so her independence grows alongside her friendships.

Comparing

She watches a couple walk past, hangs out with Tombo, and starts measuring her life against everyone else’s. Being able to live on your own isn’t the same as being happy about it.

The pie

She battles a terrible oven and the weather to get it there, and the person who gets it doesn’t care. She misses the party too. It doesn’t explain losing her flight by itself, but it adds to the mood.

01:00:00

About 1:12:30

Kiki says out loud that she’s losing confidence. Up to here the film only showed it.

Flying was her job, her independence and her identity, all at once.

So when it fades and her broom snaps, she’s losing way more than a skill. Jiji suddenly only meows too. Miyazaki never explains the rules, so all we can do is read what the film shows us.

04Finding a way四

Ursula has been here before.

She’s a painter, and she tells Kiki she hated everything she made until she stopped forcing it.

Magic isn’t just a handy skill for Kiki, it’s who she is as a witch. Losing flight makes her wonder who she even is without it. Ursula’s paintings stopped feeling like hers too, until she found her own style instead of copying other people.

Neither can be switched on whenever you want. Ursula treats it as a normal, temporary thing, and she doesn’t hand Kiki a fix. The scene just changes how Kiki sees the problem.

Stepping away.

Without flight she can’t run the delivery service, and for a while nothing moves forward at all. Independence felt amazing at the start and now it just feels heavy. Stopping shows her that being independent doesn’t mean always being productive or carrying everything yourself.

At Ursula’s cabin there are walks, rest and no pressure to fly or paint. A break won’t bring her powers back by itself, but it’s a gentler way to get through burnout.

She’s not suddenly fine on her own either. Osono, Ursula and Tombo all help. Growing up seems to be about understanding yourself differently while still being part of a community.

05What comes back五

Her old broom is gone. She flies on a borrowed one.

At the airship climax Tombo gets caught in the accident and Kiki rushes to help, on a street sweeper’s broom that’s nothing like her own.

At the start

Flying looks easy. It’s just part of her day, and she’s excited about being on her own.

At the end

A wobbly take-off on a broom that isn’t hers, but she keeps trying because someone needs her.

drag to compare

The film never says exactly why her magic comes back. It seems to line up with a change in how she sees things.

What came back

Her flyingBut now she uses it to help someone else, not just to prove herself.
Her place in KorikoShe built it through loneliness and doubt, not before them.

What stayed changed

Jiji’s voiceHe stays with her. They just talk differently now.
Her old confidenceThe rescue doesn’t erase what she went through.

Jiji stays, but the talking doesn’t.

One explanation, credited to producer Toshio Suzuki, is that Jiji is basically another version of Kiki, so his silence shows she’s growing up and doesn’t need him in the same way. It could also just feel like losing something that mattered. Growing up changes relationships, and not every change feels good. (I’m going with the original Japanese ending here. Some English releases changed the last Jiji scene.)

Jiji’s silence
ORICON News ↗

The film finishes with Kiki writing home and a quick montage of the people and places she’s connected to. We don’t find out her whole future. She’s doing well and still learning, and that’s enough for it to feel finished.

Recovery and change can both be true.

So, does a happy ending have to restore what was lost?

Not really. Kiki gets her flying back, but not her old self and not her old way of talking to Jiji. The ending works because it makes room for both.
